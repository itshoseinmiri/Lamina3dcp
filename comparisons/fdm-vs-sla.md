# FDM vs SLA (Resin) 3D Printing

> FDM extrudes molten thermoplastic filament layer by layer, while SLA cures liquid resin with light — FDM is fast and durable for functional parts, SLA delivers finer detail and smoother surfaces.

FDM (Fused Deposition Modeling) and SLA (Stereolithography) are two of the most widely used 3D printing technologies. FDM melts and deposits a plastic filament along a path to build each layer. SLA is a resin process that uses a laser or light source to selectively cure liquid photopolymer in a vat. Both are additive, but they differ sharply in resolution, material behavior, and workflow.

## Comparison table

| Dimension | FDM (Filament) | SLA (Resin) |
|-----------|----------------|-------------|
| Process | Extrudes molten thermoplastic layers | Cures liquid photopolymer resin with light |
| Cost | Low material and machine cost | Higher resin cost; more consumables |
| Speed | Fast for larger, simpler parts | Fast for fine detail; slower for bulk volume |
| Accuracy | ~0.1-0.3 mm; visible layer lines | High resolution, ~0.025-0.1 mm; smooth finish |
| Materials | PLA, ABS, PETG, nylon, composites | Standard, tough, flexible, castable, dental resins |
| Ideal use | Functional prototypes, jigs, durable parts | Detailed models, miniatures, dental/jewelry masters |
| Limitations | Layer lines, anisotropy, lower detail | Brittle parts, messy post-cure, UV sensitivity |

## Pros and cons

**FDM**
- Pros: low cost, durable functional materials, large build volumes, simple and clean workflow.
- Cons: visible layer lines, weaker between layers (anisotropy), limited fine detail.

**SLA**
- Pros: excellent surface finish and detail, isotropic layers, ideal for smooth or intricate parts.
- Cons: resin handling and post-processing (wash and UV cure), often more brittle, higher running cost.

## When to choose which

Choose **FDM** for functional prototypes, brackets, jigs and fixtures, and larger durable parts where cost and toughness matter more than fine detail. Choose **SLA** when you need smooth surfaces, crisp fine features, or high dimensional detail — for example presentation models, dental and jewelry patterns, or small intricate components. Many workshops keep both: FDM for everyday functional work and SLA for detail-critical parts.
