# Additive vs Traditional Manufacturing

> Additive manufacturing builds parts directly from digital models with no tooling, while traditional methods — injection molding, casting, and machining — excel at high-volume, low-cost production once tooling is amortized.

"Traditional manufacturing" covers established, mostly formative and subtractive processes such as injection molding, die and investment casting, and machining. Additive manufacturing (3D printing) is a newer, digitally driven approach that builds parts layer by layer. The core trade-off is tooling: traditional methods need molds, dies, or fixtures that cost time and money up front but produce parts cheaply at scale, whereas additive skips tooling entirely and stays economical at low volumes.

## Comparison table

| Dimension | Additive Manufacturing | Traditional Manufacturing |
|-----------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| Process | Layer-by-layer build from CAD | Molding, casting, or machining with tooling |
| Cost | No tooling; flat cost per part | High tooling cost; very low unit cost at volume |
| Speed | Hours to days, no lead time for tooling | Weeks for tooling, then very fast per part |
| Accuracy | Moderate, process-dependent | High and highly repeatable at scale |
| Materials | Polymers, resins, metals, composites | Vast: metals, thermoplastics, alloys, ceramics |
| Ideal use | Complex, custom, low-volume, prototypes | Standardized parts at medium-to-high volume |
| Limitations | Slower per part at scale; finish/anisotropy | Tooling cost, lead time, design constraints |

## Pros and cons

**Additive manufacturing**
- Pros: no tooling, fast iteration, complex geometry, mass customization, on-demand and low-waste.
- Cons: higher per-unit cost at scale, surface finish, size and material limits.

**Traditional manufacturing**
- Pros: lowest unit cost at volume, proven repeatability, mature material and finish options.
- Cons: expensive tooling, long lead times, costly design changes, geometry constraints (draft angles, undercuts).

## When to choose which

Choose **additive** for prototypes, custom or one-off parts, complex geometries, and low-to-medium volumes where avoiding tooling saves cost and weeks of lead time. Choose **traditional manufacturing** when volumes are high enough to amortize tooling, when maximum repeatability and lowest unit cost matter, or when a specific certified material or finish is required. The break-even point shifts with part complexity and quantity — additive often wins below a few hundred to a few thousand units.
